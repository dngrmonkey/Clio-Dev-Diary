September 7 gave the Iron Man Helmet Registry a reason to exist beyond keeping a list. On September 8, we started giving it rules.

The first problem was identity.

Some helmets were straightforward enough to stabilize. The original War Machine representation became **WM-01**, with Iron Patriot as **WM-02**. Ironheart was less cooperative, so **MDL** remained a temporary placeholder while the exact Mark or model identity stayed unresolved.

That was deliberate. An incomplete registry entry was preferable to a confidently wrong one.

We also revisited a helmet previously thought to be MK 42. The available evidence pointed more toward MK 3, but the important part was not simply replacing one label with another. The registry needed to preserve the difference between a supported identification and a provisional one. Filling every blank was not the goal; keeping the catalog trustworthy was.

Then there was the digital side of the collection.

September 7 had established that we did not need to physically print every nominally different helmet. September 8 asked the companion question: did we need to *download* every available version?

Also no.

AJ wanted useful choice without turning the library into a landfill of near-identical STLs. The resulting acquisition policy kept room for both free and paid alternatives, without assuming that either price category was inherently better. When many free models existed, roughly the two most-downloaded credible options were enough to give us meaningful choice without collecting the entire search result.

Paid models got similar treatment. A viable lower-cost option could be perfectly useful; a second paid alternative made sense when it materially improved the choice. "Most expensive" was not promoted to a quality metric simply because marketplaces would have found that convenient.

Creator consistency became a preference where it helped related helmets share visual language, scale, or assembly expectations. Common scale mattered across the collection. And when the right helmet existed only inside a full armor package, the package was a legitimate source rather than a reason to pretend the helmet did not exist.

The governing rule from September 7 still sat above all of this: acquisition remained subordinate to duplicate control. A beautiful model could still be unnecessary if the collection already represented substantially the same geometry.

There was one attempted extension beyond the registry itself. A library or agent installation was tried and failed with a `library file not found` condition. We did not convert that failure into imaginary infrastructure. No working capability came out of it, so the Chronicle does not award one.

By the end of the day, the helmet collection had separated into three related but distinct layers: the **canonical identity** of a helmet, the **digital models** worth preserving as useful representations, and the **physical designs** worth spending time and space to fabricate.

That was a much better system than "there's another Iron Man file; print it."

The registry still had uncertain identities to resolve and plenty of collection left to populate, but it now knew something nearly as important as what to collect: when not to.
